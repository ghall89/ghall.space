The Curse of the Magic Probability Machine

Oct 22nd, 2026
Learning Life Tech

Over the last couple years, I’ve started getting into crossword puzzles. Puzzmo got me hooked, and then more recently I picked up a couple books to do as well. This week I thought it might be a fun little project to learn how to construct my own puzzle, and learn about the process.

I found this software called Ingrid that was pretty easy to get the hang of, and I’ve solved enough crossword puzzles to mostly be able to reverse-engineer some “best practices”, but I wanted to learn more.

There were some cool resources from various places like the New York Times, and on YouTube. But, what should have been the start of a fun journey to learn something new turned into a bit of an existential crisis thanks to the dreaded initialism: “AI”.

The clearly AI-written write-ups were bad enough, but the amount of resources suggesting the use of AI instead of actually learning something, with titles like “How to Make Your Very Own Crossword Puzzles With ChatGPT”, felt frighteningly dystopic. I could have just ignored it, and moved on, but, as someone who greatly values curiosity and learning, it really served as a stark reminder of how much “fuck it, just use AI” content is out there.

Sure, I could probably create dozens of puzzles a day with AI, but what would be the point? What would I have learned? How to craft the perfect prompt for the magic probability machine to barf out something that reasonably resembles a crossword puzzle, become a 100x puzzle builder, and maximize my ROI or some such bullshit?1

Sometimes, the point of doing something is just to do it. I think that’s what the people enthralled by the promise of AI have forgotten, or perhaps never realized in the first place.

On some level, I get it. I can’t draw, and while I’d love to be a decent illustrator I know I don’t have the drive to dedicate the time or energy it takes to get good at that craft. I’m not ashamed to admit I’ve used AI to realize some of my ideas, but I will never share them, and they have no value to me – I’m pretty sure they ended up getting deleted at some point. In contrast, silly little blog posts like this has infinitely more value to me. Even if nobody reads it, even if it’s not very good, it’s the culmination of everything I’ve written and posted online for almost 25 years2, and a stepping stone towards whatever I write next. The challenges, the failures, the many instances of poor writing3 and, most importantly, the lessons they imparted along the way is the “end goal”.
